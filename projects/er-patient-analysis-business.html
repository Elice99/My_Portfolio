ER Patient Analysis: what the report shows

A one-page Power BI report on emergency room visits: volume, waiting time, satisfaction, referrals and who comes in. This page covers what it shows, what to be careful with, and what I would fix next.

The problem

This project uses a synthetic demonstration dataset. Nothing here describes a real hospital, and none of it should be used for real patient-care decisions.

Emergency departments keep an eye on patient volume, waiting time, patient experience, referrals and admin processes. Those usually sit in separate reports, so it is hard to see how they move together across dates, weekdays and weekends, age groups, gender and referral destinations.

I built a one-page Power BI report that puts them in a single view. It is a monitoring and exploration tool. It helps you ask the first questions. It does not explain why anything happens.

The short answer

This is a monitoring report, not an investigation with a verdict. The most useful things it taught me were about the data itself: most visits have no satisfaction rating, the ages look randomly generated, and the monthly trend is shaped partly by which months the data covers.

What I found

01

Most visits have no satisfaction rating

How far can I trust the satisfaction average?

The report shows 75.10% of visits as not rated. The 5.47 average therefore describes only about a quarter of visits.

A direct count of the source file gives different figures: 72.7% blank and an average of 4.99 on the scores present. One explanation fits both gaps. If some visits carry a score of 0, DAX drops them from the average and counts them as not rated, because a comparison to BLANK() is also true for 0. About 222 zero scores would explain both numbers. Counting the zeros in the file is the first thing to check.

What it means. Either way, the average describes a minority of visits. It should not appear without the share of visits that were not rated next to it.

Conclusion

Show the response rate beside the average, and settle what a score of 0 means.

02

Most visits show no referral

Where do patients get referred?

The report shows None as the referral for 5,400 of 9,216 visits (58.59%). The other 3,816 visits (41.41%) have a referral department.

What it means. General Practice and Orthopedics make up about 74% of the referrals that do exist. None is a real value in the data. Whether it means no referral was needed or nothing was recorded is a question for the data owner. A direct profile of the file read those same 5,400 rows as blank, so it is also worth checking whether None is literal text in the CSV.

Conclusion

Confirm what None means before calling 58.59% unreferred.

03

The ages look randomly generated

Who comes to this ER?

Adults account for 7,106 of the 9,216 visits (77%). That sounds like a finding, but the Adult group covers ages 19 to 79 while Infancy covers only ages 1 and 2, so the bars are not comparable.

Divide each group by the number of years of age it covers and the picture changes.

What it means. Every group gets about 113 to 120 visits per year of age. Real ER data is usually not that flat, because very young children and older adults tend to stand out. This looks like randomly generated ages, which fits a synthetic dataset.

Conclusion

Compare age groups per year of age, not by bar height. Done that way, there is no age effect at all.

04

Weekdays and weekends look the same per day

Is the ER busier on weekdays?

The report shows about 6.6K visits on weekdays and 2.6K on weekends, which looks like a big gap.

Weekdays are five days and weekends are two. 6.6K over five days is about 1.3K a day, and 2.6K over two days is also about 1.3K a day.

What it means. The totals compare unequal numbers of days, so they suggest a pattern that is not there. It is another sign of evenly spread, generated dates. These figures come from the chart's rounded labels, so treat the per-day numbers as approximate.

Conclusion

Compare day types per day, not in total.

05

The monthly line mostly reflects which months are in the data

Is there a seasonal pattern?

The monthly chart peaks at 1,024 visits in August and bottoms out at 431 in February. It rises steeply between February and April and drops after October.

The data runs from 1 April 2019 to 30 October 2020. April to October appear in both years. January to March appear only in 2020, and November and December only in 2019. Months covered twice naturally add up to more visits.

What it means. So most of that shape is coverage, not seasonality. The yearly totals have the same problem: 2019 has 4,338 visits and 2020 has 4,878, and both are partial years, so the gap is not a full-year comparison.

Conclusion

Compare like with like: use year and month together, or only the months both years cover.

06

The administrative flag splits almost evenly

What share of visits carry the administrative flag?

4,612 visits are flagged TRUE (50.04%) and 4,604 are FALSE (49.96%). Together they add up to all 9,216 visits, so no visit has a missing flag.

What it means. Nobody has written down what the flag means, so I cannot say whether it relates to scheduled or unscheduled care.

Conclusion

Define the flag before reporting it as administrative appointments.

What I would do next

01

Confirm what None means for referrals

High priority
Evidence
The report shows 58.59% of visits with a referral of None, and a direct file profile read those rows as blank.
What to do
Ask the data owner what None means, write it in the data dictionary, and show Not recorded separately if blanks and None ever appear together.
What I expect
Stops 58.59% being read as unreferred when it might mean something else.
02

Reconcile the satisfaction numbers and show the response rate

High priority
Evidence
The report shows 5.47 and 75.10% not rated. A direct count gives 4.99 and 72.7% blank.
What to do
Count the zero scores in the file, decide whether 0 is a real rating, and put the response rate on the report next to the average.
What I expect
The average becomes honest about the quarter of visits it describes.
03

Confirm the wait time unit

High priority
Evidence
The average is 35.26 on a range of 10 to 60, with no unit stated.
What to do
Ask the data owner and label the card.
What I expect
The KPI becomes something a manager can act on.
04

Clarify whether patient_id is a visit or a person

Medium priority
Evidence
There are 9,216 unique IDs in 9,216 rows.
What to do
Confirm the grain, and rename the measure Total Visits if each row is a visit.
What I expect
Avoids reporting a row count as a patient count if repeat visits exist.
05

Fix the date handling and month sorting

Medium priority
Evidence
Month-only summaries mix the same month across years, and the data covers partial years.
What to do
Mark the Date table, switch off the automatic date tables, and use a year and month field.
What I expect
Time comparisons stop being shaped by coverage.
06

Simplify the model to one patient table

Medium priority
Evidence
Two similarly shaped patient tables are joined by patient_id with filtering in both directions, and the reason is not documented.
What to do
Confirm what the second table is for, and remove it if there is no reason.
What I expect
Less duplicated data and fewer ambiguous filters.
07

Add definitions, visible filters and clear labels

Medium priority
Evidence
The report carries no metric definitions, and gender is shown as coded values.
What to do
Add a short definitions panel, show which filters are active, and label coded values in plain words.
What I expect
More trust and easier use for a management audience.

What this can't tell you

Every record is synthetic, so no conclusion here describes real hospital performance.

The satisfaction and referral fields have large gaps or unclear values, as covered above.

The meaning of the administrative flag and the unit of the wait time are not specified in the source file.

The data covers part of 2019 and part of 2020, so year and month comparisons are not like for like.

The model contains two similarly shaped patient tables, and the reason for that design is not documented.

The formulas come from the project files, and the figures come from the report screenshot and a profile of the source CSV. Report interactions were not re-run in Power BI Desktop for this write-up, and the refresh schedule, row-level security and published settings were not checked.
