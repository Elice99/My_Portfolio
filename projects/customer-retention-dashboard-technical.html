The solution

This page describes my Customer Retention workbook. Every figure quoted here I reconciled against the raw CSV with a direct profile; the dashboard and the data agree row for row.

SheetWhat it holdsWhy it is there
01 Churn-DatasetThe raw 7,043 rows, 21 columns, as loadedThe single source of truth; nothing else in the workbook edits it
PivotTableThe pivot layer: counts, shares, sums and band tablesEvery dashboard figure computes here, so a refresh updates everything at once

Three layers, one direction of data flow: raw data feeds the helper columns and pivots, pivots feed the dashboard and analysis sheets. Nothing on the dashboard is typed: every card and chart reads pivot output, and the workbook's only arithmetic sits in the helper columns and share formulas documented below. That single rule is what makes the workbook safe to refresh.

The data and the pivot layer

The dataset is the Kaggle Telco Customer Churn table: 7,043 customers, 21 fields covering demographics, services, account configuration, charges and the churn flag. I loaded it to the data sheet clean, with SeniorCitizen as 0/1 and TotalCharges numeric, including the 11 whitespace values that coerce to blank.

The pivot sheet carries the measures the dashboard reads. The core ones:

MeasureDefinitionDashboard use
At-risk customersDistinct count of customerID where Churn = Yes (1,869)The headline KPI card
Ticket loadSum of tech and admin ticket counts, split by churn group (2,173 / 885)The two ticket cards
Monthly billing at riskSum of MonthlyCharges where Churn = Yes ($139,131)The monthly charges card
Lifetime billingSum of TotalCharges where Churn = Yes ($2,862,927)The yearly-charges card (see the label note below)

Tenure bandsA helper column bands tenure in years (F/12 through an IF ladder): under 1 year 29%, out to 6 yearsThe subscription-time bars

One labelling lesson I want on the record: the $2,862,927 card reads Yearly Charges, and that label is wrong. The field it sums is TotalCharges, the cumulative amount each churned customer paid across their whole stay. The monthly card ($139,131) annualises to about $1.67M, which is the honest forward-looking figure. I keep the card because the magnitude is the point, and I fix the label in the recommendations, but I document it here first.

The formulas, as the workbook carries them

Three kinds of formula work live in this workbook, and they tell you where the thinking happens: helper columns on the data sheet, share arithmetic on the pivot sheet, and none at all on the dashboard, where every card and chart reads pivot output directly. I read the formulas back out of the saved file, so what follows is exactly what is in it.

Data sheet: the helper columnsTEXT
X2  =IF(W2="Yes", 1, 0)         // churn flag as a 1/0 column

Y2  =F2/12                      // tenure months -> years

Z2  =IF(Y2<1,"<1 Year",
     IF(Y2<2,"<2 Years",
     IF(Y2<3,"<3 Years",
     IF(Y2<4,"<4 Years",
     IF(Y2<5,"<5 Years",
     IF(Y2<=6,"<6 Years", ""))))))   // the tenure bands the dashboard charts

The X column turns the churn text flag into a numeric the pivots can sum, the Y column converts tenure to years, and the Z column is the band ladder behind the subscription-time panel. One edge worth recording: the ladder is a strict less-than at every rung except the last, which uses <=6, so a customer at exactly 72 months lands in <6 Years. No story changes, but a rebuild should make the rungs symmetric.

Pivot sheet: shares and the churn-rate checkTEXT
AA2  =SUM(X:X)/COUNTA(W:W)*100      // overall churn rate -> 26.54%

K77  =I77/I79*100                   // group count over base, as a share
F78  =E78/E79                       // churned share of the base -> 26.5%
C81  =$C$78/E79*100%                // churn-rate check cell

The pivot sheet mixes PivotTables with a small hand-built summary block, and the hand-built part is where the percentage arithmetic lives: group counts over the 7,043 base, and the churn-rate check that recomputes 1,869 over 7,043 as a cross-check on the pivot's own count. That double path (pivot count versus formula ratio agreeing) is a cheap integrity test I would put in every workbook.

Visual grammar, panel by panel

PanelVisualThe decision behind it
KPI cardsSix large numeric cards across the topThe five-second read: scale, ticket load, billing. Largest type on the screen because these are the numbers the meeting is about
DemographyGender doughnut plus four stat chipsDeliberately calm. It answers who the base is, and its flatness is part of the story: nothing demographic is driving the risk
Subscription timeHorizontal bars, under-1-year on top at 29%Ordered by tenure band so the front-loaded risk window reads top-down at a glance

Internet serviceThree-slice pie with DSL, fiber, no-service chipsThree categories, comparable shares; the fiber plurality is the point

My visual rules on this build: one idea per visual, shares labelled directly on the marks instead of in a legend hunt, horizontal bars wherever category names are long, and doughnuts reserved for two-way splits. The demography panel uses muted greys and blues so the KPI cards stay the loudest thing on the page.

The two-level slicer design (contract, gender, internet service) maps to the three questions a retention lead actually asks: which contracts, which customer profile, which product tier. Anything more granular belongs in the CustomerRiskAnalysis sheet, not on the executive screen.

If I took it further

  1. Relabel the lifetime-billing cardChange Yearly Charges to Lifetime Billing and add an annualised card beside it, so the two readings are both on screen and both correct.
  2. Add the churn-rate pivotA rate-by-contract visual turns the mix panels into risk panels and kills the mix-versus-rate misreading.
  3. Wire a live data sourcePoint the data sheet at a scheduled extract so the monthly review runs on current data, with the pivot layer doing what it was built for.
  4. Merge the two projects' viewsBring the churn model's score distribution onto the analysis sheet, so the dashboard shows not just who churned but who is next.
  5. Templatise the shellThe sheet architecture (data, pivots, welcome, dashboard, analysis) is reusable for any executive KPI workbook; package it once and reuse it.

What this project shows

This dashboard and my churn model are the same investigation for two audiences, and building both taught me the translation layer matters as much as the analysis: the model's 0.858 ROC-AUC and the dashboard's 1,869 are the same story in two languages.

The habits I would carry forward: build the KPI layer on pivots so refreshes propagate, label figures for what they actually measure rather than what sounds right, put the calm panel first when it clears the ground for the loud one, and reconcile every card against the raw rows before calling the dashboard done.
